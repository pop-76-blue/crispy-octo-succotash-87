Charles Babbage & Early Computing

Unit 1  ·  History segment for the quiz — the Analytical Engine vs. Difference Engine No. 2, dates, and contemporaries

Charles Babbage (26 December 1791 – 18 October 1871) was an English mathematician, philosopher, and mechanical engineer. He held the Lucasian Chair of Mathematics at Cambridge — the same position later held by Stephen Hawking, and centuries earlier by Isaac Newton. Babbage is often called a "father of the computer" not for one single invention, but for two very different machines he designed across his lifetime: the Difference Engine and the Analytical Engine.

Difference Engine (No. 2) vs. Analytical Engine

This is the distinction the quiz will ask about directly — they are not the same machine, and only one of them is a "computer" in the modern sense.

Difference Engine No. 2Analytical Engine
What it isA mechanical calculatorA general-purpose, programmable mechanical computer
Designed1847–1849, as a refined version of his original 1820s Difference EngineConceived in 1834; worked on until his death in 1871
What it could doOnly addition, repeated according to the "method of finite differences," to build mathematical tables (e.g. for navigation)Any calculation set before it — addition, multiplication, conditional branching, looping
Could it be reprogrammed?No — built for one jobYes — instructions and data were entered on punched cards, borrowed from the Jacquard loom
Key design ideaUses only arithmetic addition, avoiding the harder mechanics of multiplication/divisionSeparated the "Store" (memory) from the "Mill" (the part that processed numbers) — the same split modern computers still use between RAM and a CPU
Was it built in his lifetime?No — Babbage never built it himself; the Science Museum in London completed one from his original drawings in 2002No — only a small portion (the mill and a printer) was ever assembled, completed posthumously in 1910

Contemporaries

Ada Lovelace (Countess of Lovelace)

Translated and annotated an Italian engineer's paper describing the Analytical Engine, adding notes that included what's now recognized as the first published algorithm intended to be carried out by a machine — which is why she's often credited as the first computer programmer.

Luigi Menabrea

An Italian engineer (later a general and prime minister of Italy) who published the original paper describing the Analytical Engine — the paper Ada Lovelace translated and expanded with her own notes.

John Herschel

A close friend of Babbage's from Cambridge and a noted polymath astronomer. Together they founded the Analytical Society as students, promoting a more modern style of calculus.

George Peacock

A mathematician and the third founding member (with Babbage and Herschel) of the Analytical Society at Cambridge.
