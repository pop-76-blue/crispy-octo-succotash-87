Lab 8d & 8e — Why Voltage Divides the Way It Does

Unit 3  ·  Practice on measuring: a resistor + 2 bulbs vs. 3 bulbs, all on 9V

Lab 8d puts two series groups side by side across the same 9V battery. Sub-circuit 1 is one resistor plus bulb 1 and bulb 2. Sub-circuit 2 is bulbs 3, 4 and 5. Every bulb is 48Ω. Lab 8e then asks you to explain what you measured. The key idea is that in a series group, voltage splits in proportion to resistance — the same current flows through every part, so a bigger resistance takes a bigger share (V = I × R).

Lab 8e, question by question

1. Total voltage drop in the resistor + bulb 1 + bulb 2 group?
By Kirchhoff's Voltage Law the drops in a series group add up to the supply: 8.12 + 0.39 + 0.39 = 8.90 V, which is about the 9 V battery. The small gap is a measurement/rounding difference — the law says it should be 9 V.
2. Is the same true for bulbs 3, 4 and 5?
Yes. 2.97 × 3 = 8.91 V ≈ 9 V. Both sub-circuits are in parallel across the battery, so each one's drops must add up to 9 V.
3. Why is bulb 1's drop so much smaller than bulb 3's?
Same current flows through everything in a series group, so voltage splits by resistance. In sub-circuit 1 the resistor is far larger than a 48Ω bulb, so it takes nearly all of the 9 V and leaves only a sliver for each bulb. In sub-circuit 2 there are three equal 48Ω bulbs, so each takes an equal third: 9 ÷ 3 ≈ 3 V.
4. Voltage drop across the resistor and bulb 1 together (calculation and measurement)?
Theory (KVL): 9 − Vbulb 2 = 9 − 0.39 = 8.61 V.
Measurement: add the two readings you took, 8.12 + 0.39 = 8.51 V, or put one multimeter across both. Write down both numbers and note they agree closely.
5. Find the resistor from the measurements, then read its colour bands.
Current: bulb 1 is 48Ω with 0.39 V across it, so I = V/R = 0.39 / 48 = 0.008125 A (8.125 mA). The same current flows through the resistor.
Resistor: R = V/I = 8.12 / 0.008125 ≈ 999 Ω ≈ 1000 Ω (1 kΩ).
Colour bands: 1000 = 10 × 102 → brown, black, red (check the fourth band on your resistor). With a gold band (±5%) the range is 950 Ω to 1050 Ω. You can confirm the bands with the Resistor Color Code Decoder.
