Significant Figures

Also called "sig figs" — a way of showing how precise a measurement is.

Every measurement has some uncertainty. Significant figures are the digits in a measurement that are actually known with confidence — measured or reasonably estimated. A recorded value like 7.90 cm tells you more about the precision of the measuring tool than 8 cm does, even though they're close to the same number.

The Rules

  1. Digits 1–9 are always significant.
    e.g. 369 has 3 significant digits.
  2. Zeros between two significant digits are always significant.
    e.g. 3007 has 4 significant digits.
  3. Zeros placed before other digits (leading zeros) are never significant.
    e.g. 0.0032 has 2 significant digits.
  4. Zeros placed after other digits, and after a decimal point, are significant.
    e.g. 7.90 has 3 significant digits.
  5. Zeros at the very end of a number with no decimal point are ambiguous. We can't tell if they were measured.
    e.g. 8000 might have 1, 2, 3, or 4 significant digits depending on how it was measured.
  6. Every digit in scientific notation is significant.
    e.g. 5.900 × 103 has 4 significant digits — this is why scientists prefer scientific notation for ambiguous cases like rule 5.

Sig Figs in Calculations

Multiplying / dividing: your answer can only have as many significant digits as the least precise number in the calculation.

0.21 × 1603 = 336.63 → 0.21 has 2 sig figs, so round to 3.4 × 102

Adding / subtracting: your answer can only show as many decimal places as the measurement with the fewest decimal places.

3.2 + 3.19 − 2.007 = 4.383 → 3.2 has only 1 decimal place, so round to 4.4
