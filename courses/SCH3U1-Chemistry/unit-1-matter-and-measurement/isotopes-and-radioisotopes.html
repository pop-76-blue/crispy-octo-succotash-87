Isotopes & Radioisotopes

Unit 1, Lesson 3 · Same element, different mass — and what happens when the nucleus is unstable.

Isotope notation

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same number of protons) with a different number of neutrons — so they have different masses.

AZX
A = mass number (p⁺ + n⁰)
Z = atomic number (p⁺)
X = element symbol
A neutral atom always has electrons = protons. Neutrons = mass number (A) − atomic number (Z).

Radioisotopes

An unstable isotope (too many or too few neutrons for a stable nucleus) is a radioisotope. It decays — spontaneously releasing particles/energy to reach a more stable arrangement.
RadiationWhat it isStopped by
Alpha (α)Heaviest, most highly charged particle (a helium nucleus)Paper, or even a few cm of air
Beta (β)A fast-moving electron, negatively charged~0.5 cm of aluminum
Gamma (γ)Pure energy (a wave) — no mass, no chargeThick lead or concrete

Average atomic mass

The mass shown on the periodic table is a weighted average of all naturally occurring isotopes of that element, based on how abundant each one is. See the Average Atomic Mass page for the interactive calculator and practice.

Applications

Medical tracers & PET scans

A radioisotope (e.g. Fluorine-18) is attached to a molecule the body uses naturally (like glucose) and injected. As it decays, it reveals where that molecule accumulates — useful for spotting tumours.

Radiotherapy

Targeted radiation (e.g. Co-60) destroys malignant cells.

Dating artifacts

Carbon-14 decays at a known rate, letting scientists estimate the age of once-living material. See the Half-Life page.
