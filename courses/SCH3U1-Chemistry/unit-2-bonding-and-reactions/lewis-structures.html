Ionic bonding

Octet rule: atoms lose or gain electrons to reach a full outer shell (8 electrons — stable, like the nearest noble gas). Ions get square brackets and a charge; curved arrows show the electron transfer.

Example: sodium (1 valence e⁻, EN 0.9) + fluorine (7 valence e⁻, EN 4.0) → sodium transfers its one electron to fluorine. Both end up with full shells: Na⁺ and F⁻, giving the formula NaF.

Covalent bonding

"Sharing is caring" rule: however many electrons an atom needs to complete its octet, it shares that many. A shared pair = one bond (one line). Single bond = 1 line, double = 2 lines, triple = 3 lines. Unshared pairs on an atom are called lone pairs.

Drawing complex Lewis structures

  1. Calculate TVE (total valence electrons) — add each atom's valence electrons; subtract electrons for a positive charge, add for a negative charge. TVE must come out even.
  2. Put the atom with the lowest electronegativity (usually first in the formula, or with the most unpaired electrons) in the centre. Place a single bond from it to every other atom.
  3. Give every outer atom a full octet using lone pairs.
  4. Count the electrons used. If you've used exactly TVE, you're done (push lone pairs in to fill the central atom if it's short). If you have leftover electrons, add them to the central atom.
  5. If it's an ion, add square brackets and the charge.
Exceptions: Be and B can be stable with fewer than 8 electrons around them. Any atom from period 3 or lower can hold an expanded octet (more than 8).

Formal charge

FC = (valence electrons of free atom) − (lone pair electrons) − (bonding electrons)

Formal charge helps pick between multiple valid-looking Lewis structures. The best structure has formal charges as close to zero as possible, with any negative charge on the most electronegative atom.

Resonance

Some molecules (like the nitrate ion, NO₃⁻) can be drawn with more than one valid Lewis structure that only differ in where the double bond sits — these are resonance structures, not different molecules. In reality, the extra pair of electrons is delocalized (spread across the whole structure), so all the bonds end up the same length — in between a single and a double bond. We connect resonance structures with a double-headed arrow (↔) to show this.
