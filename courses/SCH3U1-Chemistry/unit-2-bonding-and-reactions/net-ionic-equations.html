Molecular, Complete Ionic & Net Ionic Equations

Unit 5: Chemical Reactions · Three ways to write the same precipitation reaction.

When two ionic solutions are mixed and an insoluble solid forms, that's a precipitation reaction and the solid is the precipitate. Whether a combination is soluble or not comes from a set of solubility rules (reference table below).

The three equations

Example: mixing potassium chloride and lead(II) nitrate solutions.

1. Molecular equation— every substance written as a whole compound, with states:
2 KCl (aq) + Pb(NO₃)₂ (aq) → 2 KNO₃ (aq) + PbCl₂ (s)
2. Complete ionic equation— split every aqueous compound into its ions. Leave solids and liquids (like water) whole:
2 K⁺(aq) + 2 Cl⁻(aq) + Pb²⁺(aq) + 2 NO₃⁻(aq) → 2 K⁺(aq) + 2 NO₃⁻(aq) + PbCl₂ (s)
3. Net ionic equation— cross out spectator ions (ions unchanged on both sides) and keep what's left:
2 Cl⁻(aq) + Pb²⁺(aq) → PbCl₂ (s)
Spectator ions are the ions that appear in the same form on both sides of the complete ionic equation — they don't actually participate in forming the precipitate, so the net ionic equation leaves them out.

Practice

For each pair of reactants, write out all three equations (balanced, with states), then reveal to check.

Try this: before revealing the first practice problem, predict which ions will be spectators — the ones that stay dissolved and don't end up in the precipitate.
