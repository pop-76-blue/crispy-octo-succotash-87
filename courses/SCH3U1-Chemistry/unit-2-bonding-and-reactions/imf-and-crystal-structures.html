Intermolecular Forces & Crystal Structures

Unit 4: Bonding & Molecular Shape · What holds separate molecules together, and how solids pack.

Intramolecular forces (bonds) hold a molecule together — strong. Intermolecular forces (IMFs) hold separate molecules to each other — much weaker, but they're what control melting point, boiling point, and physical state.

The three IMFs

London dispersion

Present in every molecule (polar or nonpolar) — temporary, shifting dipoles. More electrons/bigger molecule → stronger London forces. The only IMF in nonpolar molecules.

Dipole-dipole

Between the permanent positive end of one polar molecule and the negative end of another. Only in polar molecules.

Hydrogen bonding

A strong special case of dipole-dipole: H on one molecule attracted to O, N, or F on another molecule. Not a true bond, but ~10% the strength of one.

Strength: London dispersion < dipole-dipole < hydrogen bonding (weakest to strongest). Stronger IMFs mean more energy is needed to separate molecules — so higher melting and boiling points.

Crystal structures

Solids with very regular, repeating particle arrangements are crystalline. How they're classified depends on what's doing the "holding together":

A closer look: carbon allotropes

Allotropes are different structural forms of the same element.

Diamond

Tetrahedrally bonded carbon network. Hard, nonconductor, transparent, very high mp.

Graphite

Flat hexagonal sheets (trigonal planar carbon) held together by weak London forces between layers. Soft, conducts (delocalized e⁻ within a layer).

Graphene

A single sheet of graphite. Extremely strong, highly conductive, one atom thick.

Fullerene

Hollow carbon sphere/ellipsoid (e.g. the C₆₀ "buckyball") of hexagons and pentagons. Very stable.

Nanotube

A rolled cylinder of graphene. High tensile strength, conducts electricity.
